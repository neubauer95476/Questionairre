The Boundary Questionnaire

A self-reflection on where your boundaries hold, where they thin out, and where they may be missing.

This questionnaire has 135 statements about your mind, heart, social life, sense of meaning, and body. Answer honestly with how often each one is true for you.

There's no right answer. Extreme answers (Always / Never) and moderate ones (Often / Seldom) reveal different things than a balanced Sometimes — that's the point.

Proposal for a Personal Boundaries Assessment Product

by Mia Lobel

The following is a proposal for developing and marketing an interactive, multimedia product focusing on the concept of "Personal Boundaries." A pivotal human requirement, basic to the understanding of the physical and psychological "selves," the concept of personal boundaries is an integral part of all our prevailing psychological constructs; it is associated with self esteem, with productivity and achievement, with overall life satisfaction, with the ability to cope socially, mentally, financially.

The product is a multimedia demonstration of what a "Personal Boundary" is, and how it may be managed effectively. It is a tool that translates the responses of the user into an immediate visual and experiential animation which depicts the impact of the responses chosen on the user's personal boundaries. This instrument, which points to more effective alternative responses, is a method of assessment and intervention that is unique in the field.

The Market

Some venues for the distribution of the proposed software are:

  • Educational: schools, libraries
  • Therapeutic: counseling, testing and assessment centers, self-help shelves
  • Business: in-house testing and training of all employees who interface with customers
  • Organizational Development: in-house testing and training
  • Internet: a "Self Help Room," where people would pay for various assessments and products offered, or for the time spent on site using the products, with membership for various packages made available

The Concept

Boundaries differentiate us from that which surrounds us, by sorting the perceptual field into the "Me" and the "not Me." As a psychological concept, it is integral to healing the ravages of codependency, addiction, sexual abuse and incest. In the field of Organizational Development, human boundary issues are at the forefront of any interaction between humans.

This product is constructed to address the following characteristics:

  • Dishonesty (denial, projection, delusion)
  • Negative affect (frozen, distorted or recycled feelings)
  • Control issues (low stress tolerance, violent tendencies, rigid orientation to change)
  • Cognitive dysfunctions (ego oriented, irrational, obsessive, confused)
  • Perfectionism (either/or thinking, unrealistic expectations)
  • External referencing (other directed, impression management, shame based reactions)
  • Dependency issues (low productivity, impaired judgment, low initiative)
  • Fear (rigidity, judgmentalism, depression, narcissism, stasis, negativism)
  • Loss of personal morality (compromised value system, loss of spiritual base)

The Product Introduction

The client is first introduced to the concept of Personal Boundary by listening to a guided imagery explanation of the concept, accompanied by interactive graphics. This introduction is designed to connect clients to their own experience and begin the process of learning and internalizing new concepts. Notions of healthy and unhealthy boundaries are explored, and the client is shown how to use this instrument not just for assessing individual standing, but also for learning the new approaches needed to restore their boundaries to a more effective configuration.

Picturing Boundaries

Throughout the experience, the screen displays the condition of the user's boundaries. An avatar is surrounded by rings (or, in this version, a cylindrical brick wall) of different colors and textures. Each ring or band represents an interpersonal need, and as the user responds to the questions, its appearance changes.

The rings may get thinner and paler, eventually showing gaps — these represent "holes" where the person's boundaries are undefended, weak or nonexistent. The rings may get thicker and darker, forming "walls" where the person's boundaries are overly defended, locked in or inflexible. Although unattainable, the ideal boundary is one without holes or walls.

Questions

A set of questions dealing with thoughts, feelings and behaviors focuses on one facet of the individual at a time:

  • Body — physical, medical
  • Mind — cognitions, ideations, beliefs
  • Heart — affect, emotions
  • Social Relations — social interactions, social context
  • Spirit — existential beliefs, spiritual orientation, ethics

Each question also relates to one developmental dimension, adapted from William Schutz's FIRO model of interpersonal needs:

  • Inclusion (I) — belongingness, membership, significance, acceptance, on an In/Out continuum
  • Control (C) — competency, influence, productivity, decision making, conflict management, on a Top/Bottom continuum
  • Openness / Intimacy (O) — openness, honesty, sexuality, love, on a Close/Far continuum

The questions are clustered into 15 diagnostic areas — one for each combination of facet and developmental dimension.

Results

As each diagnostic area is completed, a Short Profile, based on the range of scores, is generated. Once the whole questionnaire is completed, a Long Profile is generated. All the profiles have a predictive tone, and range over three levels of functioning: Moderate, Needs Work, and Critical.

The following is an example of three Short Profiles for the three levels of functioning, when assessing an individual's Inclusion needs in the Social Relations facet:

A) You feel involved in your relationships. You cope effectively in social situations and usually get your needs met without harming others. You find yourself mattering to others and you are able to enjoy long term, loyal, interdependent relationships.
B) Whether you find yourself on the outside looking in, or trapped on the inside longing to get out, you are conflicted about your significance in relationships. You are having difficulties finding a suitable "crowd" and may substitute addiction for unresolved dependency needs.
C) Either you wish to be "in" with everyone, terrified of being left out, or you withdraw from everyone, dreading yet craving to be noticed. In any case you are suffering, and may try to allay it with addiction to some substance, behavior or person.

Resource/Help Section

The Long Profile contains summary data for all the diagnostic areas. It encourages users to make the necessary changes and suggests general directions in seeking help, with short exercises and techniques for turning limitations into strengths. These "how to" steps may be practiced, tested out, and role-played by choosing different answers on the questionnaire, thereby impacting the user's boundary in new, observable and tangible ways — a continuing testimony to the existence of choice, and to how awareness of choice is integral to effective Personal Boundary Management.

About this version

This web app is an implementation of the vision above: a self-guided version of the assessment, with a 3D avatar and a cylindrical, color-coded brick wall standing in for Ms. Lobel's rings, built from the same 135-question instrument, five life facets, and three developmental dimensions (Inclusion, Control, Openness) described here.

Boundary Concept Description

The Spiel — a guided visualization

This is currently presented as text. A recorded audio narration of this guided visualization is planned for a future version of the app.

Think of yourself surrounded by an imaginary demarcation, with which you designate your "personal space" — perhaps a tricolor ribbon, akin to the rings of Saturn, that encircles you, creating a space which is analogous to your internal sense of how much "space" you take up. Now, visualize this space as a safe place, and you are sitting in it. Take a deep breath.

In there, you are supreme commander; in there, there is no one but you — your thoughts, your feelings, your ideas, your goodies, your shortcomings, all that is the essence of YOU, your "YOU-ness" of you. You bring this "YOU" into the world, and that is no more negotiable or remarkable than the fact that you brought your hands with you, as well; what you do with all this is another matter. The point here is that it is there, a given, with all its potentials and limitations.

On this level we are not much different from a carrot seed. That seed has the potential, the "wiring," to become the best carrot it can be. If it's planted in fertile soil and raised under optimal conditions, it will achieve its inherent, optimal "programming." If it's planted in rocky, arid soil and grown in a hostile environment, it will still do its best: it will grow around the rock, it will extract from its surroundings what it can, and even if it ends up looking different than it could have, it still strove toward the closest approximation of its blueprint, in order to complete itself in its "creation" and in its given nature.

I believe I am not much different from that carrot seed, except I can reflect on my own "carrotness," or move to a different garden and arrange for my own watering, once I become aware that my potential is being compromised.

We each bring this unique "Me-ness" into the world with us at the moment of our birth, when we are at the height of our potential but also at our most fragile — our selves pure, still untouched, our boundaries intact. And then life happens.

At our most helpless, life begins to impact our boundaries, and some of it strengthens and affirms the self, and some of it begins to shred and rearrange "that which defines the self": our boundary. And as we grow up, holes and walls are created as a means of coping with and surviving the pains and disappointments of life, which in turn affect who we are, how we live, with whom, and when.

In "real life," the shapes, sizes, locations, and proximity of the space the self claims will vary from individual to individual and from context to context. You will also note that in three dimensions, there is a space between each other's boundaries that may vary in size, but it is a space that is "not Me and not You." This is a Neutral Zone, and it is here that effective, healthy communication takes place. I define this as "you heard what I meant to say, and you let it be about me and not you."

Further, imagine our communications — every word I say to you, and vice versa — to be visible as an object, let's say a piece of tissue like Kleenex, and every time I open my mouth and say something, you could observe where my "words" float to, where they are placed in the Neutral Zone by the sender and by the receiver.

This is what you would typically observe: when my boundary is intact relative to the topic of our discussion, and you place a message into the Neutral Zone between us, I am able to lean over, so to speak, look at your communication, and respond to YOU — by empathizing, paraphrasing, building on what you said.

If my boundary has a "hole" in it, when it is damaged relative to a particular issue or person, I have no protection, no means of "detachment" from what occurs around me or from other people's thoughts, needs, or wants. Because there is no boundary, no barrier there, I'll suck in your communication right into my head and heart, because it already resonates in my being as if it were my very own, as if it were actually about me. It is what I tell myself all the time. I make it about me, because it is familiar, and because I don't have the means to separate "it" from "me" or from "you." While it may only happen to be a similar issue or concern that I'm dealing with, I will personalize it and make it my own. It is when I have a "hole" in my boundary that there is literally no separation between me and your words.

It is predictable, then, that I'll think or feel: "this is all about me." For example: if I feel myself to be an interesting, brightly entertaining person, then my boundary vis-à-vis this issue can be said to be intact. I feel good enough, safe in this aspect of the self, so when you come along and say, "Oh, I feel bored!", your words settle into the Neutral Zone. I lean over my intact boundary, look at what you said, and respond, maybe by saying: "Oh, poor you, that must be awful. I was bored once too." This response is in a "listening to you" mode.

Conversely, if I feel I am not a very interesting person, I constantly worry others may think me stupid, and I feel responsible for entertaining you. So when you say, "Oh, I feel bored," your words will enter my being, point to my worst fears, and settle into me, experienced as a stab, a horrid unmasking event. In this case, I am no longer listening to your communication, but instead I am busy personalizing, taking away from what you have said. This is no longer a response; this is a reaction to my own fears.

Another way to illustrate this point is as follows: typically, you were born with two hands, and as time went by, you became more and more proficient at controlling them. Now imagine if, as a small child, your caretakers had managed to convince you that you actually had three hands — two which you can control from "inside" of you, while the third can only be controlled by controlling another person who is actually attached at that hand. Now imagine that third hand to be a toxic hand, one that hits, demeans, punishes, one that needs to be predicted in order to avoid some pain. It would stand to reason that in such a case, I would devote a lot of energy to scanning, studying, and tracking that person, becoming an expert on their process, so I can anticipate their reactions and manage their thoughts and feelings accordingly. It would also make sense that the more effort I put into getting to know and manage your thoughts and feelings, in order to keep your hand from hurting me, the less time I will spend getting to know myself and what it is I want or need.

Caretakers convincing us that we have a third hand may sound like: "No, you don't think that, you should not feel this, don't cry, don't be angry, you are just like him or her, you must, should, have to be like this and not the way you are," and so on.

Another process which takes place, whether caretakers are toxic or not, is that we all want to resolve our inclusion needs. As a result, from the beginning we strive to develop a façade which is appreciated and approved of by those around us. If in my household smiling is what gathers safety, I will learn to paint and keep a smile on my façade even if it kills me. However, if I do not genuinely feel that smile, and you come and say, "My, what a lovely, smiling person you are," I will not be able to take that feedback in. What I would think instead is something akin to: "Yeah, if you only knew how miserable I feel, what a fake I am." Sadly, a façade will become the only face after a while, the real self hidden behind a wall of unknowing.

Not being known and seen as we know and see ourselves is the cause of most human suffering.
